Thought Records & Cognitive Distortions

Thoughts aren't facts. A thought record helps you slow down a painful thought, look at it from several sides, and find a view that's both kinder and more accurate.

This comes from cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). It isn't about "thinking positive". It's about thinking accurately, so your feelings fit what's actually happening.

1How thoughts shape feelings

Something happens. Almost instantly, a thought pops up about it, often so fast you don't notice it. That automatic thought is what drives the feeling and the urge that follow, more than the event itself.

SituationA friend hasn't replied to your message all day.
Automatic thought"She's annoyed with me. I always mess things up."
Feeling and bodyAnxious 70, sad 50. Tight chest, stomach drops.

The same situation with a different thought ("she's probably busy at work") leads to a very different feeling. A thought record helps you find the thought, notice its traps, and check it against the evidence.

2The seven columns

  1. Situation: just the facts. Who, what, where, when. What a camera would have recorded.
  2. Emotions: name each feeling (sad, anxious, angry, ashamed…) and rate how strong it is, 0–100. Notice where you feel it in your body.
  3. Automatic thought: what went through your mind? Find the "hot thought", the one carrying the most emotion. Rate how much you believe it, 0–100.
  4. Thinking traps: which distortions show up in the thought? (See the 12 thinking traps.)
  5. Evidence for: facts that support the hot thought. Facts, not interpretations or feelings.
  6. Evidence against: facts that don't fit the thought, or that it leaves out.
  7. Balanced thought: a fairer, more complete thought that takes in both columns. Then re-rate how much you believe the old thought and how strong the feeling is now.
A drop from 80 to 60 is a real success. The aim is to loosen a thought's grip, not to make the feeling disappear.

3Questions to ask a thought

  • What's the evidence for this? What's the evidence against it?
  • Am I confusing a thought, or a feeling, with a fact?
  • What would I say to a friend who had this thought?
  • Is there another way to explain what happened?
  • What's the worst that could happen? The best? The most likely? Could I cope with the worst?
  • Will this matter in a week? In a year?
  • What does believing this thought do for me? What would change if I let it go a little?
  • Am I using one of the thinking traps?

4Start with the body

A strong feeling is easier to work with once your body has settled a bit. If you're above about 7 out of 10, ground first: feel your feet, lengthen your out-breath, or tap Sploot now. Then come back to the thought record. Noticing where a feeling lives in your body (a tight throat, a heavy chest) also helps you find the hot thought behind it.
